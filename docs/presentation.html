The Triadex Muse: deterministic composition machine — a SuperCollider reimplementation of the algorithmic music composer designed by Edward Fredkin and Marvin Minsky at MIT (1969). Presentation notes for 30-minute talk with live demonstration.

_I. context

The Triadex Muse was designed by Edward Fredkin (computer scientist) and Marvin Minsky (AI researcher) at MIT in 1969, commercially released by the Triadex company in 1972. About 280 units were ever built. It is one of the first algorithmic music machines: a device that composes, not synthesises.

The Muse contains no randomness. Its entire output is determined by eight slider positions and an initial state. Yet it produces melodies that sound composed, surprising, and varied. This paradox sits at the centre of the instrument's interest.

Maryanne Amacher used the Muse extensively during her MIT fellowship at the Center for Advanced Visual Studies (1972-1976). She created her unpublished Tone and Interval Studies (18 quarter-inch tapes, 1976-78) using Minsky's Muse, and documented her psychoacoustic experiments in the Additional Tones workbook (Workbook IV, 1976/rev. 1987).

_II. architecture

Everything in this section describes the original hardware as designed by Fredkin and Minsky. The architecture, the counters, the shift register, the XNOR feedback, the selector matrix, the pitch table — all of this is the 1969 circuit. My SC implementation reproduces this logic faithfully, verified against Tillman's reverse engineering and the patent.

On each clock tick the Muse executes five operations: (1) read theme slider sources, compute XNOR feedback; (2) advance binary counters; (3) on every second tick, advance the shift register; (4) read interval slider sources to form a 4-bit note value; (5) map through an 8-note scale. Counter sources produce periodic patterns at powers-of-two rates. Shift register sources produce aperiodic patterns from XNOR feedback. The interplay between these two source types is where the music originates.

Source: Edward Fredkin & Marvin L. Minsky, US Patent 3,610,801 (1971)

_II-b. sound

The original Triadex Muse produced a square wave through a divide-down oscillator. A single frequency, 50% duty cycle pulse, sent directly to a small built-in speaker. No filter, no envelope, no amplitude shaping, no portamento between notes. The timbre was absolutely fixed: every note had identical harmonic content (odd harmonics at 1/n amplitude rolloff). The only variation was pitch and rhythm.

The sound was raw, buzzy, and digital in character. Notes clicked on and off with no transition. At low tempos each note rang as a steady tone; at high tempos the sequence became a rapid chirping. The built-in speaker added its own colouration, but the electrical signal itself was a pure square wave at whatever frequency the resistor network selected.

This austerity is part of the instrument's identity. The Muse makes no attempt to sound like anything other than what it is: a binary state toggling a voltage level. All musical interest comes from the sequence, not the sound. The fact that listeners hear melody, phrasing, and development in a stream of identical square wave tones is itself evidence for the strength of the underlying algorithmic structure.

Original: square wave from a divide-down oscillator, fixed timbre, built-in speaker.
My extension: the faithful synthesis type reproduces this (band-limited Pulse at 50% width). The six other synthesis types (sine, saw, FM, pluck, DPOAE, enhanced) are my additions — they let the same deterministic sequence inhabit different timbral surfaces.

_IV. scales

The original hardware used a resistor network divider producing fixed intervals. This implementation replaces the network with a configurable 8-value float array, enabling microtonal precision. The interval of equivalence (scale[7]) is not hardcoded to 12 semitones. For Bohlen-Pierce it is 19.02 (the tritave); for Carlos Alpha it is 5.46 (non-repeating). The D bit adds this value, whatever it is.

37 scale presets are included. The same slider configuration sounds fundamentally different in each scale. Where does the Muse's musicality reside? In the logic, or in the tuning?

_V. sync

Multiple Muse instances share a single TempoClock. One instance drives tempo; others are tempo-locked and can be offset by fractional beats. Each instance has independent slider configuration, scale, synthesis type, and base frequency. The shared clock ensures the internal counters advance simultaneously.

This is the core of Amacher's Muse practice: two synced units producing tones in near-consonant relationships designed to trigger psychoacoustic phenomena in the listener's ear. Independent SR states, aligned clocks, incommensurate slider configurations produce emergent interlocking melodies without explicit counterpoint.

Original: the hardware had a sync jack — two physical units could share a clock. This was a feature of the product.
My extension: the shared TempoClock, tempo-locking, fractional beat offsets, independent base frequencies, and the ability to run arbitrary numbers of instances are all SC additions. The underlying principle — shared clock, independent shift registers — is the same.
_VI. Amacher

Amacher's Additional Tones workbook (Workbook IV, 1976/rev. 1987) documents systematic experiments with psychoacoustic phenomena: difference tones (F_d = F_2 - F_1), beats of mistuned consonances (near-octave, near-fifth, near-fourth), and fundamental tracking. She operated the Muse at approximately 3,000 Hz for "standing wave phenomena" (her term) and processed the output through a Bode Ring Modulator. The "Well Tuned Ear" range is 800-6,000 Hz.

The DPOAE synthesis type in my implementation directly connects to Amacher's practice: it generates two-tone stimuli at ratio ~1.22, optimal for evoking the cubic difference tone 2f1-f2 in the listener's cochlea. This is my addition — the original hardware had no such mode, but the psychoacoustic use was Amacher's.

The Amacher papers (approximately 200 boxes of notebooks, scores, tapes) are held at the New York Public Library for the Performing Arts. The 18 Tone and Interval Studies tapes (1976-78) were made with the Muse. The notebooks likely contain the slider configurations she used. No technical analysis of these materials has been published.

_VI-b. reverse
engineering

I attempted to recover Amacher's slider configurations directly from the album recordings (Head Rhythm 1, Chorale 1, Synaptic Island) by extracting pitch sequences from the audio and matching them against all possible Muse configurations. The search space is 408 = 6.55 trillion possible slider settings, reduced by separating the theme search (404 = 2.56 million) from the interval search.

The inference engine was verified correct: given a clean signal from the emulator, it recovers the known preset (tested with "Scale"). The pipeline included multiple F0 extraction methods (pYIN, CREPE neural pitch tracker, zero-crossing analysis), onset-based and grid-based note segmentation, gap-based pitch clustering, relative contour matching, and Monte Carlo search with hill climbing across all 32 possible starting offsets in the counter cycle.

Result: zero solutions found across all recordings, all methods, all offsets.

The failure is not in the inference engine but in the audio. The recordings are the end of a long signal chain:

config space408 = 6.55 trillion slider configurations
x offsets32 possible counter starting positions
x scaleunknown (may not be default major)
x ring modBode Ring Modulator destroys 1:1 pitch mapping
x roomalbum tracks, not line-out recordings
x 2 voicestwo synced Muses in the Chorale pieces

Key finding from spectral analysis: all detected F0 values in the isolated excerpt (sequence_one_run.wav) were 2200-3000 Hz. Initially assumed to be harmonics of a lower fundamental, this was confirmed by Amacher's own workbook as the intended operating range: "standing wave phenomena w/ 3,000 Hz Muse business." The ring modulator then creates non-harmonic sidebands, which is why no pitch tracker (pYIN, CREPE, zero-crossing) achieves meaningful confidence on the recordings.

The NYPL archive remains the realistic path. If Amacher's notebooks contain the slider positions, the problem collapses from 6.55 trillion unknowns to a lookup. The inference pipeline is ready for any clean, unprocessed Muse recording.
